Beyond the Circle & Hold

Three alternative interaction paradigms for dispatching to AI on a calm, distraction-free canvas

Starting Point

Woodpecker OS currently uses a single interaction to bridge the human thinking space and the AI execution space:

Circle content + hold 500ms → Magic Wand fires

This works well, but it's the only gesture. We explored three radically different approaches — each optimized for a different kind of human-AI conversation.

Describe the tongue of a woodpecker
— Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1510

Research Foundations

These three paths are informed by extensive research across calm technology, paper-digital interaction design, and intentional AI interfaces. Key findings that shaped the exploration:

Calm Technology

Amber Case's principle: technology should "inform and encalm" using peripheral cues. IDEO's "ambient revolution" thesis: as AI becomes more capable, calm design becomes more critical, not less. E-ink's slow refresh rate is a design feature, not a bug.

Paper-Digital Bridging

CHI research (PapierCraft) found that gesture vocabularies mirroring paper annotations — circling, underlining, drawing arrows — have near-zero learning curve. Liza Daly's interactive marginalia research validates margin-based AI responses as "least disruptive for users."

Intentional AI Friction

Research shows "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking." The AI Flywheel framework identifies four types of productive friction — including "invitations to expertise" that create mandatory pause points before AI responds. Handwriting itself is a form of beneficial friction.

Light & Presence Metaphors

In yogic practice (Trataka), the candle flame serves as a "single, steady point of visual focus" that reduces mental noise. Modern calm lighting installations use "breathing" animations as living presence. The flame is warm, impermanent, requiring gentle attention — it cannot be rushed.

Sources: IDEO Edges, Calm Tech Institute, CHI 2024 PaperTouch, PapierCraft (CHI), Liza Daly's Interactive Marginalia, AI Flywheel framework, DK Consulting cognitive friction research, Marie Claire Dean's Slow Design principles, Mindvalley Trataka research

Path 1

The Margin River

Inspired by 800 years of manuscript marginalia

Medieval scribes left wide margins for annotations, corrections, and commentary. Over centuries, marginalia became a rich tradition — readers would converse with texts, leave questions, draw connections. The margin was the thinking space beside the thinking space.

In Woodpecker, the canvas gains a visible right margin — a gentle vertical line dividing your writing space from the AI's territory. Content in the main area is yours alone. To dispatch to AI, you draw an arrow toward the margin, or write directly in it. Responses appear as elegant marginalia, connected back to their source.

Step-by-step interaction flow on the canvas

Why This Feels Calm

The spatial separation is key. Your thinking happens on the left. AI knowledge lives on the right. There's a physical boundary — the margin line — that you consciously cross when you want help. You never accidentally summon AI; you have to deliberately push content across the river.

The responses are small. Marginalia is compressed, dense, efficient. It doesn't dominate your thinking space. It annotates it.

◐
E-ink optimization: The margin line can be a single-pixel vertical rule with a slightly different background shade — essentially free on e-ink. Responses render as static text. The dashed margin line works beautifully with e-ink's high contrast. No animation required.

Strengths

  • Deep cultural precedent — 800+ years of marginalia
  • Clear spatial separation of human and AI thinking
  • Responses are compact, non-intrusive
  • Natural for e-ink: static layout, no animations
  • Can have different margin zones (top: questions, bottom: actions)
  • Preserves the main canvas as a sacred space

Tensions

  • Limits canvas to left-to-right writing (constrains free sketching)
  • Margin width is finite — long responses need special handling
  • Arrow gesture detection is harder than circle detection
  • May feel constrained for users who sketch everywhere
  • Infinite canvas scroll complicates the margin positioning

Technical Feasibility

  • Gesture: detect arrow-like strokes (directional, x-velocity > y-velocity, ending near margin boundary). Extend gesture-detection.ts with detectArrowGesture()
  • Margin zone: overlay a semi-transparent div at the right edge of the viewport. Track canvas-to-screen coordinates for the boundary.
  • Responses: reuse existing HandwrittenTextShapeUtil but constrain x-position to margin zone. Add maxWidth prop for compact text.
  • Connection lines: reuse existing arrow bindings to visually link marginalia back to source content.
  • Complexity: Medium — margin boundary tracking and arrow gesture detection are the main new work.

Path 2

The Candle

Light it to think. Extinguish to reflect.

A small candle icon lives in the corner of the canvas. It's the bridge between solitary thinking and AI collaboration. But unlike the circle-and-hold, the Candle enforces a minimum thinking time. You can't fire-and-forget. You must sit with your thoughts first.

When you tap the candle, it "lights" — a warm, subtle glow appears at the edge of the canvas. The AI is now silently observing everything you write. It doesn't interrupt. It doesn't suggest. It watches. When you're done thinking, you tap the candle again to "extinguish" it. After a contemplative pause of 2-3 seconds, the AI delivers its reflection — informed by the full context of everything you wrote while the candle was burning.

Candle flow with key insight: burn duration affects response depth

Why This Feels Calm

The Candle inverts the typical AI interaction. Instead of "ask → answer", it's "think → observe → reflect". The user is in control of the duration. A quick question gets a short burn (30 seconds). Deep thinking gets a long burn (5+ minutes). The AI has more context for longer sessions, naturally producing more thoughtful responses.

The deliberate pause after extinguishing is crucial. It creates a moment of stillness between the human's thinking and the AI's response. Like the moment between asking a wise friend a question and them taking a breath before answering.

Optional depth mechanic: The burn duration could map to response depth. <30s = quick fact. 1-3min = contextual answer. 5+ min = deep, multi-faceted reflection. This creates an incentive to think longer.

◐
E-ink optimization: The candle flame is one of the few elements that could use e-ink's partial refresh for a subtle flickering effect (updating just the candle region). The observation border can be a simple static dashed line. The timer updates once per second — a single small region refresh. Very e-ink friendly.

Strengths

  • Enforces minimum thinking time — can't fire-and-forget
  • AI gets richer context from full observation session
  • Beautiful metaphor: illumination, contemplation, wisdom
  • Fits Da Vinci theme perfectly (candlelit study)
  • No canvas constraints — write/sketch anywhere
  • Burn duration → response depth is an elegant mechanic
  • Very low UI footprint (single icon)

Tensions

  • User must remember to extinguish — forgetting wastes tokens
  • Can't target specific content (whole canvas is observed)
  • No clear way to say "do this" vs "explain this"
  • Real-time observation may feel surveillance-like to some
  • OCR/capture of writing must happen continuously
  • Responses always appear at same location (less spatial)

Technical Feasibility

  • Candle UI: persistent <button> in the tldraw overlay layer. Toggle between lit/unlit CSS states.
  • Observation: on extinguish, capture the entire visible canvas via editor.toImage(). Send as vision prompt to Claude Code.
  • Continuous capture: optionally screenshot at intervals (every 30s) during observation to provide temporal context ("you started with X, then shifted to Y").
  • Response placement: position below the lowest visible content, similar to current magic wand behavior.
  • Complexity: Low-Medium — simpler than margin detection. Main new work is the candle state machine and periodic capture.

The Underline Lexicon

Your pen already knows the vocabulary

People have been annotating paper for centuries. A single underline means "important." A double underline means "very important." A wavy line means "questionable." A strikethrough means "delete." These conventions are universal — taught in every school, used in every language.

The Underline Lexicon maps four distinct pen gestures below text to four different AI behaviors. No buttons. No mode switching. No chrome. Just pure pen-and-paper vocabulary that happens to summon intelligence.

The four gesture types: Remember, Explore, Execute, Forget

Why This Feels Calm

There is zero UI chrome. No buttons, no toolbars, no mode menus. The interaction vocabulary is entirely pen-based and maps to gestures people already know. A single underline beneath "Meeting at 3pm" to remember it. A double underline beneath "quantum entanglement" to explore it. A wavy line beneath "email the client" to execute it.

Each gesture has a distinct motor pattern that's nearly impossible to confuse accidentally. A single straight line feels different from two quick lines, which feels different from a wavy squiggle. This is important for preventing false triggers on a device meant for calm.

The "remember" gesture is especially powerful. It lets you build up context over time without asking a question. By the time you do ask (via double underline), the AI already knows your meeting is Thursday, your budget is tight, and Dave prefers email over Slack.

◐
E-ink optimization: This is the most e-ink native approach. All interactions are pen strokes — the primary input on e-ink. Recognition feedback is a simple line style change (no animations). Responses render as static text. The entire experience could work with zero page refreshes for the gesture recognition phase. Only the response text triggers a partial refresh.

Strengths

  • Zero UI chrome — pure pen interaction
  • Maps to existing paper annotation habits
  • Four distinct intents from one gesture vocabulary
  • "Remember" builds AI context passively over time
  • Each gesture has a distinct motor pattern — low false positives
  • Most e-ink native of all three approaches
  • Content-targeted — AI knows exactly what you're referring to
  • Can coexist with the existing circle-and-hold gesture

Tensions

  • Gesture recognition for underline types is non-trivial
  • Works best for text — less natural for sketches/diagrams
  • User must learn the vocabulary (though it's intuitive)
  • Need OCR to know what text is above the underline
  • Distinguishing "two quick strokes" from "one slow stroke" is hard
  • Doesn't support multi-content queries (circling multiple items)

Technical Feasibility

  • Gesture detection: extend gesture-detection.ts to analyze stroke properties. Key signals: y-variance (low = horizontal), x-span (wide = underline), number of segments, waviness (y-oscillation frequency).
  • Text association: when underline detected, find the nearest content shapes above it using editor.getCurrentPageShapes() filtered by y-position.
  • Wavy detection: compute standard deviation of y-coordinates relative to the regression line. Wavy lines have high y-variance with regular periodicity. Use FFT or simple zero-crossing count.
  • Double detection: detect two near-parallel strokes drawn within 500ms of each other. Track last stroke timestamp and compare geometry.
  • Complexity: Medium-High — the gesture classifier is the most sophisticated new component. But it builds directly on existing analyzeForEnclosingGesture patterns.

Synthesis

Recommendation: Compose, Don't Choose

These three paradigms aren't competing alternatives — they're complementary layers that serve different moments in the thinking process. The most powerful version of Woodpecker would offer all three, each emerging naturally when the user needs it:

The Underline Lexicon is the foundation. It's the most natural, lowest-friction way to interact with AI from a pen-first interface. Single underlines passively build context. Double underlines satisfy curiosity. Wavy underlines dispatch actions. This should be the first to implement because it extends the pen vocabulary rather than adding UI.

The Candle is the philosopher's tool. When you want to think freely — sketch, mind-map, wander — and then receive a holistic reflection, light the candle. This is unique because it doesn't require you to specify what you want help with. The AI observes the arc of your thinking. Implement second as the "deep thinking" mode.

The Margin River is the scholar's tool. For users who want a persistent, spatial separation between their thinking and AI's knowledge — a reading-and-annotating mode rather than a free-sketching mode. This could be a page layout option rather than a global feature — "ruled page with margin" vs "blank canvas".

Together with the existing circle-and-hold (the quick, targeted dispatch), these four paradigms would create a remarkably rich yet calm interaction vocabulary:

When you want to... You... Metaphor
Ask about specific content Circle it + hold Magnifying glass
Remember / explore / act / forget Underline with the right stroke Pen annotations
Think freely, then get reflection Light the candle, write, extinguish Candlelit study
Have a persistent thinking + reference space Use a margined page layout Scholar's manuscript

The key principle across all of these: the user always initiates. The AI never interrupts. The AI never suggests. It waits. It observes. It responds only when summoned — and only through deliberate, physical gestures that feel like extensions of the pen, not departures from it.

This is how we compete with the phone. Not by being faster, but by being better at being slow.
